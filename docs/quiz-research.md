# Research: a more precise, more interesting interest questionnaire for UniPath

Numbers in [brackets] refer to the Sources list (pages actually opened). "(search)" = only seen in a web-search snippet, not opened.

## 1. Interest models, instruments, licences
- Holland's RIASEC: six types (Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional) on a hexagon where neighbours correlate more than opposites; "now pervades career counseling research". Alternative structures (Tracey and Rounds octagon/sphere) fit some cultures better, so the hexagon is a useful frame, not a law. [1]
- UniPath's ten traits map well onto RIASEC: tehnic+miscare ~ R; logic+stiinte ~ I; creativ ~ A; oameni+ingrijire ~ S; business ~ E (and C, organising); societate+limbaj sit between S, E and A. No trait is "Conventional" (data/order) except partly business and logic. Our own mapping, not from a source.
- O*NET Interest Profiler: free self-assessment of the six RIASEC types; Mini-IP has 30 items for mobile; 10-20 minutes for the full tool. [2]
- O*NET licence: the Interest Profiler / Work Importance Locator are under a separate "Career Exploration Tools" licence with two options. CC BY-ND 4.0 forbids adapting the items; the O*NET Tools Developer License allows modifying them if you credit USDOL/ETA, validate the modified product and state that USDOL did not approve it. The O*NET database itself is CC BY 4.0. [3][4] Conclusion: NOT public domain. Do not copy or translate its items without the developer licence; ideas only.
- O*NET Work Importance Locator (work values) was retired on 3 June 2024; archived materials only. [5] The page did not list the six values; treat "O*NET work values" as background only (could not verify).
- Open RIASEC: Open Psychometrics offers a 48-task test using "RIASEC Markers from the Interest Item Pool" (Liao, Armstrong and Rounds 2008), described as a public-domain alternative to commercial inventories; rating 1-5 "enjoy"; the site itself says educational/entertainment use only. [6] The item pool is activity-based ("how much would you enjoy ..."). I did NOT copy any of its items; my draft is original wording.
- Short forms: 18REST has 3 items per RIASEC scale, alpha .68-.81 in adolescents and adults; a short Personal Globe Inventory with 4 items per scale had alpha .59-.86 (median .77) at ages 16-18 (search; opened page was blocked). Rule of thumb: 3-4 items per dimension is the floor for usable short scales; 6 per scale is common in the O*NET profiler. [7 (partial)]
- Proprietary, do not copy: Strong Interest Inventory (291 items, trademark and copyright of The Myers-Briggs Company, scored by licence) [8]; MBTI (same publisher family). [9]
- MBTI for careers: 39-76% of people get a different type after five weeks; a National Academy of Sciences review said there is not enough well-designed research to justify using it in career counselling; binary types ignore that traits are continuous. [9] Recommendation: no type labels ("you are an INTJ"); use scores per interest.
- Interests are real predictors: interests are contextualised preferences that predict choice of field, persistence, attainment and even income beyond ability and personality; they are fairly stable from adolescence on. [10]
- Romania: Euroguidance says counselling is a compulsory subject in lower-secondary since 2004, over 3,350 school counsellors, at least 1 counsellor per 800 pupils (so many students get little individual guidance, which supports a self-service tool). [11] Cedefop's Romania report lists psychological tests for interests, skills and personality in school guidance and mentions Holland-type self-assessment. [12] The new 2026 syllabus "Dezvoltare personală și consiliere în carieră" (IX-XII) asks pupils in grade IX to discover abilities, values, strengths, and lists questionnaires on qualities, abilities, values and interests as classroom activities. [13] So interest AND values AND abilities are the official vocabulary in Romanian schools. No Romanian standardised, free, open interest inventory was found (see "Could not verify").

## 2. Item and format evidence
- Activity items ("how much would you enjoy doing ...") are the standard in O*NET and Open RIASEC; the Strong also mixes occupations, subjects, activities, leisure, people, characteristics. [2][6][8] Subject items ("favourite subject") partly measure school performance and exposure, so use them sparingly. (Reasoning plus [10]: interests are contextualised, not the same as ability.)
- Interest and ability overlap only modestly (e.g. realistic/investigative with quantitative ability); ability mostly predicts performance, interests mostly predict which field you choose and stay in. So ask them separately. [10]; overlap figures from a search snippet only.
- Forced-choice (pick one of several equally attractive options) resists faking and response style better than rating scales: meta-analysis of 82 samples (106,266 people) found smaller score inflation for forced-choice, and quasi-ipsative formats resisted most; faking is smaller in real settings than in labs. [14] (Personality, not interest, data.)
- Downside: pure forced-choice gives ipsative scores (a person's scores sum to a constant), which distorts comparisons and gives low reliability with few traits; a mix with some rating items raised average reliability from .50 to .73 in simulation, and even 20% Likert items helped. [15] Design implication: UniPath's pick-one format is fine for ranking a person's own strengths (what we need: the top 3), but do not show absolute percentages like "you are 82% creative".
- Add one or two rating-style items to lift reliability and let a student say "none of these". (derived from [15])
- Gender: large average differences exist on the people-versus-things dimension (d about 0.9) and on realistic and social interests (Su, Rounds and Armstrong 2009, search snippet only). Hence: never offer an option that is "the boy answer"; make every option usable by anyone; avoid "girls help, boys build" pairings; consider showing a result even when the student's top trait is atypical for their gender. (search; item written with this in mind)
- Wording: short, concrete, everyday scenarios; one idea per option; no jargon; no school-grade wording. The O*NET page confirms activity-style, self-administered, self-interpreted design. [2] Reading-level numbers for 15-18 year olds: not verified.
- Self-efficacy and values: Romanian syllabus lists values, interests and self-confidence as separate things to explore [13]; O*NET had a separate Work Importance tool for values [5]. Recommend separate blocks rather than one blob.

## 3. Engagement
- Quiz-industry benchmarks (marketing blog, weak evidence, not teen-specific): 3-7 questions 65-85% completion, 8-15 questions 45-65%, 16+ questions 25-45%; ideal time 2-4 minutes. [16] Survey-vendor posts in search results: drop-off is steepest in the first ~15 questions and an early "danger zone" around question 3 (search).
- Tension: 20 questions will lose people versus a 7-question quiz, but reliable interest measurement needs 3-4 items per dimension (30-40 trait-hits). Mitigate: show progress, make every question a distinct scenario (variety is the engagement lever), let people stop early with a provisional result, and keep each answer one tap.
- No source on teenagers' drop-off specifically was found.

## 4. Limits and ethics
- Short tests are less reliable than 100-300 item inventories; alphas for 3-4 item scales are .6-.8 [7]. Present results as "ideas to explore", never "your career".
- Barnum effect: vague descriptions feel accurate to everyone (documented for MBTI). [9] Show concrete study domains and the reason each matched ("you chose X, Y, Z"), not personality labels.
- Cultural adaptation: hexagon structure fits imperfectly across cultures [1]; instruments need validation in the local population; O*NET developer licence itself requires validating modified items [3]. UniPath cannot validate, so state "not a validated test".
- Interests change; a 15-year-old's profile is provisional. [10] Offer a retake and suggest talking to a school counsellor. [11]

## Recommendations for UniPath
1. Use 18-20 scenario items (draft has 20), one tap each, 5 options, about 3-4 minutes. Show a progress bar and allow "see a provisional result" after 12.
2. Keep pick-one (forced-choice) because it limits faking and over-agreeing; compensate for ipsativity by (a) at least 8 main-trait hits per trait, (b) adding 2-3 "how much" rating items later, (c) showing ranks, not percentages.
3. Cover the ten traits evenly: every trait is the weight-3 option 8-13 times in the draft; each question touches 5-8 traits so no question is a tie-breaker.
4. Phrase options as activities and situations, not school subjects; cut "favourite subject" to at most one question, or drop it (grades measure ability and teaching, not interest).
5. Separate interest from confidence: add a second pass for the top 3 domains: "How sure are you that you could learn this?" (1-5). Show it as "interest vs confidence", never merge into the match score. [10]
6. Separate work values and style (3-4 items, already inside the draft: zi-de-lucru, oferta-de-munca, unde-lucrezi, ce-te-motiveaza). Better still, keep their weights low and use them only to re-rank near-ties.
7. Remove gender-coded options; test each question by swapping the imagined respondent. Avoid costly hobbies or foreign trips (done in the draft).
8. Present results honestly: "idei de explorat", reasons, a line "nu e un test validat; interesele se schimbă", a retake button, and a pointer to the school counsellor. No MBTI-style type names. [9][11]
9. Keep profile weighting as is (25% profile / 75% answers) but consider lowering to 15-20% because 12 profiles are broad; check by simulating random answers (could not test here).
10. Run the draft against the existing 40 domains with random and "pure-type" answer sets before shipping (e.g. 1000 random respondents) and check that all domains can reach the top 3; do not trust the draft until this is done.
11. Optional: shuffle option order per respondent to cancel position bias.
12. Plan to validate informally: ask the team and 10-20 students to take it twice and compare top 3 (test-retest).

## Draft notes (questions-draft.json)
- 20 questions, 4-5 options (19 with 5, one with 4), each label at most 70 characters, original wording. Item ideas only inspired by public activity-style practice [6]; no wording from O*NET, Strong, MBTI or Open RIASEC.
- Per-trait totals (sum of weights / number of weight-3 options): logic 46/8, tehnic 42/12, stiinte 36/10, ingrijire 33/9, oameni 49/10, limbaj 38/8, creativ 35/11, business 47/13, societate 36/8, miscare 32/10. Business, oameni and tehnic are slightly heavy; logic, limbaj and societate lightest on main hits.
- Tie-breakers: none by design; each question reaches 5-9 of the 10 traits (narrowest: regula-nedreapta with 5; internet-picat, the only 4-option question, reaches 6).
- Values and style questions: zi-de-lucru, unde-lucrezi, oferta-de-munca (stability vs risk, independence vs team), ce-te-motiveaza (helping vs achieving), peste-zece-ani (what impact matters), sa-inveti-ceva-greu (interest without ability).
- Cheap hobbies only; no trips abroad. Not verified: whether "oferta-de-munca" option 1 (state job maps to societate) is too crude; check with students.
- Weak spots: no Likert/rating items, no confidence block, no explicit indoor/outdoor-only question (covered by unde-lucrezi).
